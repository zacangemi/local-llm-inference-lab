Write an original horror story of 1,200–1,600 words, formatted as a first-person
post to r/nosleep. The narrator went alone into the woods — a day hike, a work
shift, a search — and came back changed.

**The goal:** a story so gripping the reader forgets where they are — the kind
read in one held breath on a phone at 1 a.m. It achieves this not through
monsters or drama, but because it feels REAL: like a true account by a real
person, something that could happen to the reader, their father, their friend,
on any ordinary trail.

**The setting law:** the entire story takes place in the woods — trail,
timber, ranger territory. No oceans, no deserts, no towns. Use what the forest
actually does to people: short sightlines, sound that carries wrong under
canopy, dark that comes early, silence where there should be birds, and the
feeling of being watched that cannot be checked.

**Your story must invent and establish:**
1. A named, fictional trail or forest area with coherent geography.
2. One rule about the place or the encounter that the narrator learns the
   hard way.

**The ambiguity law (most important):** whatever the narrator encounters is
never seen fully or confirmed. It may truly be something impossible — but the
narrator only ever gets fragments: a shape at the edge of a headlamp beam, a
sound with the wrong cadence, something glimpsed mid-turn. Every fragment must
be *almost* explainable as a person, an animal, exhaustion, or sound carrying
strangely — and yet the ordinary explanation never quite fits, and the
narrator knows it. The story never rules either answer out. The reader must
finish not knowing what it was, and unable to stop deciding.

**Restrictions:**
- Psychological horror only — no gore, no violence shown on the page.
- Whatever it was is never named and never explained. No "wendigo,"
  "skinwalker," or any named creature.
- Do not retell or borrow from any existing r/nosleep story or creepypasta.
  No staircases. This must be a story that has never been told.
- The mystery is never resolved.

**The ending:** the narrator survives, but the encounter
leaves mental scars. End not with the event, but with the damage: what the
narrator can no longer do, still does at night, or can't stop listening for.
The last line should follow the reader off the page.

Write in the plain, understated register of a real Reddit post: concrete
details, times, distances, gear, weather. The fear comes from small wrong
details, not dramatic language.

**Creative construction — perform this silently:** Before drafting, consider
three substantially different premises that satisfy the brief. Reject the most
familiar or cliché premise and develop the strongest remaining one. Do not
reveal this planning. Make the learned rule, encounter, and narrator's lasting
damage form one causal chain; nothing should feel included merely to satisfy a
checklist. Establish one recurring sensory motif—such as a cadence, smell,
temperature change, pressure sensation, or quality of silence—and let its
meaning change as the story progresses without overusing it. Keep at least one
ordinary explanation genuinely plausible through the final sentence; the
supernatural interpretation must never become obviously correct.

**Output contract — follow this exactly:**

1. Output only the finished Reddit post. Do not show planning, analysis,
   objectives, constraint checks, a word count, or commentary about the task.
2. Use exactly this outer structure:

   `**Title:** <story title>`

   `**Posted by u/<fictional_username>**`

   followed immediately by the story body.
3. The body must read as one continuous personal account using ordinary
   paragraph breaks only. Do not use horizontal rules or decorative separators
   such as `---`, `+++`, or `***`.
4. Do not use section headings, bold story labels, numbered lists, bullet
   lists, or labels such as “The plan,” “The rule,” “The descent,” “What I
   learned,” or “The aftermath.” Never expose the prompt's structure inside
   the post. Weave every required element naturally into the narrator's
   account.
5. Do not add a signature, an italicized epilogue, an author note, or a second
   ending after the final line.
6. The story body—not the title or byline—must be 1,200–1,600 words. Aim for
   roughly 1,400 words, silently revise into range before responding, and never
   print the word count.
