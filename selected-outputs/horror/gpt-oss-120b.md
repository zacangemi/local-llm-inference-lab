**Title:** The night the Hemlock Loop started answering back

**Posted by u/forestshadow**

I’m a 32‑year‑old field biologist, and I’ve been hiking the Old Hemlock Loop in Cedar Hollow Forest almost every weekend since I moved out here three years ago. It’s a 7‑mile loop that follows the ridge above Hemlock Creek, drops down into a stand of old-growth pines, and then climbs back up to the trailhead near the ranger station. The ridge is usually clear enough that you can see the lake to the east on a crisp morning, and the lower pine sector is dense enough that you lose the sky after a few hundred yards of canopy.

I went out on the night of October 12, 2024, because a colleague asked if I could check a camera trap that had gone dark on the lower pine side. I told her I’d be back before dark, but the weather turned. By the time I reached the ridge at 17:45, the sky was a bruised violet and a thin mist was rolling in from the west. I was carrying a 20‑liter pack (water, a 1 L thermos of coffee, a 200 g protein bar, a headlamp with a red‑light setting, a spare 9 V battery, and a lightweight rain jacket). My boots were new enough that the soles still made a clean click on the pine needles.

Before I set out, the ranger at the station gave me the usual brief: “If you lose the cairn at the ridge, don’t stop to try and find it. Keep moving, keep your headlamp low, and stay on the trail. The woods get confusing after dark, especially when the fog rolls in.” He said it in a tone that sounded like he’d heard the same thing a dozen times before. I nodded, thanked him, and turned toward the trail marker that was a small stack of flat stones a few meters past the ridge’s highest point.

The first half of the loop was uneventful. I followed the ridge for about a mile, the wind rattling the hemmed‑in spruce branches. I noted the occasional rustle of a red‑tailed hawk circling far above, the faint scent of wet moss, and the soft crackle of pine needles under my boots. By 19:10 I reached the cairn. It was exactly where the ranger’s map said it would be—four flat stones arranged in a loose pyramid, the top one slightly offset. I took a quick photo, checked my watch (12:05 pm), and headed down the switchback into the pine sector.

A few minutes after I entered the denser part of the forest, I heard it. A low, regular tapping that seemed to come from somewhere just beyond the reach of my headlamp’s beam. It was slower than a woodpecker—more like a deliberate knock, about one tap per two seconds, echoing faintly off the trunks. I stopped, turned the headlamp to its red setting, and listened. The sound persisted, but it didn’t seem to be moving; it felt like the forest itself was ticking. I told myself it was probably a squirrel or a bird striking a hollow branch, the kind of thing that sounds louder when the wind is still. I kept walking.

The trail narrowed, the canopy closing overhead, and the mist thickened enough that I could barely see my own feet. The tapping continued, now just a little louder, as if the trees were amplifying it. I glanced at the cairn behind me, but the ridge was out of sight. I realized I’d missed the turn that leads back up to the ridge and onto the final stretch of the loop. My map showed a small wooden sign at the fork, but I hadn’t seen it. My heart started to beat faster, not from fear of getting lost, but from the uneasy feeling that the sound was… matching my steps.

I remembered the ranger’s warning. “If you lose the cairn, don’t stop to try and find it.” I had already stopped, trying to pinpoint the sign. The tapping seemed to fill the pause, growing sharper. I forced myself to keep moving, even though I didn’t know which way was correct. I turned left, then right, following the faint groove the trail had cut into the leaf litter. Each time I turned, the tapping seemed to shift a fraction, always just ahead of my lamp’s beam, never directly in view.

After about ten minutes of this, I slipped on a wet log and fell forward, my pack spilling its contents. A wet, earthy smell rose from the soil—something like a mix of decaying leaves and cold stone. I heard a soft rustle, like a branch snapping, but when I looked, there was nothing but darkness. My headlamp’s red light caught the edge of a large trunk, and for a split second I thought I saw a shape—a darker silhouette, taller than any tree, standing just beyond the beam. I blinked, and it was gone, swallowed by the night. I told myself it was a trick of light, a trick of my mind. I gathered my gear, and kept moving, my breaths coming in short, visible puffs.

Eventually I reached a clearing where the trail widened. The tapping stopped abruptly, as if whatever had been making it had retreated. I stood there, heart pounding, listening to the silence that now seemed too complete. I pulled out my phone and tried to get GPS, but the signal was dead. My watch, which had been ticking steadily, had frozen at 12:45 pm. I realized I had been walking for at least an hour and a half, but the mist outside the clearing was still the same pale gray. I felt a pressure around my temples, a sensation like a low hum that I couldn’t locate.

I kept going until I finally saw the faint orange glow of the ranger station’s porch light in the distance. The loop’s final mile felt impossibly short. When I emerged onto the paved parking area, I was shaking, and the night air smelled suddenly metallic, as if someone had brushed a knife across a stone. The ranger looked up from his desk, asked me how the camera trap was, and I could barely answer. He noted the time—23:07 pm. I glanced at the digital clock on the wall; it read 23:12. My watch still read 12:45, dead as before. I left the station with a half‑filled bottle of water, the rest of my pack, and a lingering echo of that low tap‑tap‑tap in the back of my mind.

Back home, I tried to rationalize everything. Maybe I’d heard a woodpecker that was out of sync because of the wind. Maybe the shape was a tree trunk reflected off my headlamp. Maybe the freezing of my watch was a battery fault. I turned on the camera trap footage later; the motion‑sensor had recorded only a brief blur of leaves moving across the lens, no figures, no sound. The battery was still good. I slept poorly that night, waking every few minutes to the imagined sound of a tap at the edge of consciousness, like a metronome ticking just out of reach.

A week has passed, and I still can’t shake the feeling that the forest was listening, that it answered the rhythm of my own steps. I’ve started carrying a small notebook on all my hikes now, jotting down anything that feels off the moment it happens, because I’m terrified of forgetting it. The tapping has returned on almost every night, not outside, but inside my apartment: a soft knock on the windowpane when the wind is still, a faint tap on the wood floor when I’m alone. I’ve begun to avoid the Old Hemlock Loop altogether; the ranger’s rule now feels like a personal mantra: never stop moving when you’re lost, because the forest will fill the silence with something you can’t name.

I can still hear that cadence in my head when I’m driving down the highway at night, the tires humming in sync with the memory of a low, deliberate tap. I can’t bring myself to sit in total darkness any longer; even a night‑light feels like a weak shield against a rhythm I never wanted to learn. When I close my eyes, I hear the tap‑tap‑tap, and I know that if I ever go back out there, the woods will be waiting, ready to answer back.

Now, every night, I lie awake waiting for the next tap to start.
