**Title:** I did a solo trail sweep on Raven's Throat last month and I still can't walk on dry leaves without flinching
**Posted by u/eli_trail_vol**

I've been reading this sub for years, never thought I'd have a story to post, but last month happened and I can't stop thinking about it. I'm a part-time trail maintenance volunteer for the Blue Mountain Ranger District, usually do sweeps and clearing with a partner, but last October 12th my partner bailed last minute because his kid had a sudden fever, and the east side of the Raven's Throat Loop needed clearing before the weekend deer hunters showed up. It's a 7-mile loop off the Cascades Highway, mostly old growth Douglas fir and western red cedar, the east half cuts through the Twisted Stand, that section that burned in the 1994 Millie Fire. The canopy there is so thick you can't see more than 30 feet ahead even at noon, and it gets pitch black by 4pm this time of year. The rangers drilled one rule into us during training: if you're in the Twisted Stand and you hear footsteps that aren't yours, do not follow them. Do not leave the trail to look, do not call out again, just turn around and head back the way you came.

I left the trailhead at 1pm, overcast, 58 degrees, light wind out of the west. Pack had loppers, a pruning saw, a beat-up GPS, headlamp with fresh batteries, 2 liters of water, a peanut butter granola bar, and a thermos of coffee I'd brewed that morning. Planned to be back by 4, no big deal. First two miles were fine, cleared a couple small downed firs the storm knocked over 3 days prior, no other hikers, which is normal for a weekday mid-October.

The first weird thing was around mile 2.5, right where the trail dips into the burn. I was pushing through a low-hanging blackened branch, and I heard a boot crunch on dry oak leaves. Stopped, looked up, no one there. Figured it was a hiker ahead of me, called out "Trail's rough up here, heads up!" No answer. Waited a minute, nothing but wind in the snags, no birds—weird, because the Twisted Stand is usually full of chickadees this time of year. Kept going. The sound kept up, same pace as I was walking, 20 or 30 yards ahead. I thought maybe the hiker was moving slow, so I picked up the pace a little, and the sound did too. I stopped, the sound stopped immediately. Stood there for 30 seconds, only the wind, no crunch. Started walking again, sound started again, same distance ahead.

Around mile 3 I had to stop to cut a 6-inch fir that had fallen across the trail. Set my pack down, fired up the pruning saw. While I was cutting, I heard the crunch again, same cadence, but I was standing still. Killed the saw, listened. It was faint, coming from further up the trail, maybe 50 yards ahead. Remembered the ranger's rule then, but told myself it was just a hiker I hadn't seen through the trees. Called out again, "You good up there? Trail's blocked a bit ahead too." Nothing. Finished cutting the fir, shouldered my pack, started walking. The sound was still there, 20 yards ahead, same pace.

That's when I noticed the trail marker was missing. The USFS metal disk that's supposed to be nailed to the fir 10 yards ahead, marking the junction with the old logging spur, was gone. Thought maybe the storm knocked it off, so I kept going. A hundred yards later, the spur junction marker was there, but the main loop marker was gone again. Pulled out my GPS, it said I was still on the main loop, but the trail was narrower than it should be, more undergrowth, more dry oak leaves than I remembered. The fog had started rolling in too, thick, so I could only see 10 feet ahead.

I turned around to head back the way I came, and that's when the crunch was right behind me. Not 20 yards, not 10, like someone was standing 3 feet back, right on my heel. I spun around, headlamp beam cutting through the fog, nothing but blackened tree trunks, fog curling around the branches, no footprints, no one. Stood there for a full minute, heart hammering in my ears, listening. Nothing. Started walking back fast, and the crunch was behind me, matching my pace exactly. Every time I stopped, it stopped. Every time I walked, it walked. I didn't call out again.

I must have walked a mile back the way I came, and then I saw the trail marker for the first junction I'd passed, the one 2 miles back from the trailhead. That's when I realized I'd been walking in a circle. The fog was thicker now, I couldn't see more than 5 feet ahead, and the crunch was still behind me. Pulled out my GPS, and it was glitching, jumping between two coordinates, both of them in the middle of the Twisted Stand, nowhere near the main trail.

I don't know what made me do it, but I stopped, turned around, and yelled as loud as I could, "WHAT DO YOU WANT?" The crunch stopped. For 10 seconds, there was no sound at all. No wind, no birds, no distant highway noise, nothing. Then I heard it again, but this time it was coming from the left, off the trail, through the underbrush. Like someone was walking parallel to me, 20 feet into the woods, moving at the same speed I was. I turned my headlamp that way, and I saw a shape. Just for a second, between two snags. It was tall, lanky, like a person, but the shoulders were too high, like it was hunching, and the head was too small, no hat, no hair that I could see. It was standing still, watching me. I didn't see it move, I just saw it, and then it was gone, like it stepped behind the blackened trunk.

I didn't stick around to look again. I ran as fast as I could, loppers bouncing against my side, branches slapping my face. The crunch was behind me the whole time, faster now, like it was running too. I tripped over a root, went down hard, scraped my palm raw on a rock. Got up, kept running, and then I saw the main trail marker for the meadow at mile 4, where the trail opens up and you can see the parking lot in the distance. Ran straight, didn't stop, until I got to my truck. Didn't even lock the trailhead gate, just got in and drove. Didn't look in the rearview mirror once.

When I got home, I checked my pack: loppers, saw, thermos, all there. My palm was still scraped raw, I had a bruise on my knee from tripping, no other injuries. My GPS was dead, batteries dead even though I'd put fresh ones in that morning.

I went back to the ranger station the next day to report I'd gotten turned around, that I thought someone was following me. The ranger looked at the coordinates I gave him, shook his head. Said those coordinates were in the middle of the burn, where there's no trail at all, not even a logging spur. Said no one else reported being out there that day, no missing hikers, no one called in about a person matching the description I gave. They did a sweep of the Twisted Stand the next week, found nothing, no footprints, no sign of anyone else being out there.

The worst part isn't that I got turned around, or that I saw something I can't explain. It's the little things. I can't walk on dry leaves without my skin crawling. Last week I was in my backyard, stepped on a pile of leaves my wife tracked in on her boots, and I froze so hard I dropped the mug I was holding, hot coffee all over my shoes. I wake up at 3am sometimes, hearing that crunch, like someone's walking around the house. I check the doors, check the windows, nothing's there. I don't go hiking anymore, not even on busy, crowded trails. I can't stand the sound of a boot on leaves, the way it gets quieter when you stop, like whatever's making it is waiting for you to move again.

I don't know what it was. Could've been a hiker, a weirdo messing with people in the woods, a stray dog that learned to walk on its hind legs? No, that doesn't make sense, the shape I saw was too tall, too thin. Could've been something else, something that doesn't belong in the Twisted Stand, something that mimics sounds, that leads people in circles. I don't know. I don't want to know. All I know is that I ignored the ranger's rule, followed a sound that wasn't mine, and I'm not the same person who left that trailhead at 1pm.

I still have the trail map taped to my fridge. The section for the Twisted Stand has a big red X through it now. I don't go past that X. I don't think I ever will. I still flinch every time I hear a boot hit dry leaves, even when I know it's just my wife walking in from the car. I don't think that'll ever go away.
