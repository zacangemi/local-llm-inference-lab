**Title:** The Cedar Hollow Survey

**Posted by u/TrailMoss-42**

They still have my GPS track logged in the system at the ranger station. It shows me entering the Cedar Hollow Connector at 0647 and… well, that’s the problem. The track just ends. Not a turn back, not a pause. The last ping is at 1230, 2.1 miles in, at the old fire scar where the pines come back thick. After that, nothing. No exit, no backtrack, no signal drop that explains it. Just a dot that stops moving.

I’m not writing this to prove anything happened. I’m writing it because I can’t stop thinking about that silence, and maybe if I put it down in words someone else will recognize what I’m trying to say.

I took the Cedar Hollow Connector because it was supposed to be easy. A six-mile out-and-back along an old survey line that the Forest Service had been using for boundary checks. My supervisor wanted photos of the fire damage from last summer’s Rattlesnake Gulch blaze. Said the crew couldn’t make it, needed someone with a drone cert to get overhead shots. Standard stuff. I packed light: Nikon D850, Mavic Mini, extra batteries, water, lunch, first aid kit, and my Petzl headlamp with fresh AAA batteries. Weather was clear, 58 degrees at the trailhead. Perfect morning.

The Connector runs northeast off the Old Logging Road, starts at a pullout marked with a faded brown sign that’s half-buried in ivy. The trail itself is barely there in places—just a game corridor wide enough for a man to walk single file, flanked by Douglas fir and Engelmann spruce that grow so close their crowns meet overhead. At the fire scar, the trees are younger, planted in neat rows like someone tried to grid out a section of forest with a ruler. That’s where I stopped to launch the drone.

Everything was fine until about 1130.

I had just finished photographing the eastern face of the burn area, where the trees had been completely consumed down to charcoal stumps. The drone circled back to me on autopilot, its rotors making a sound I’d heard hundreds of times—high, steady, mechanical. I was kneeling by a partially fallen snag, adjusting my camera settings, when I noticed the birds had stopped.

That’s the first thing you notice in the woods when something’s wrong. Not a smell or a sound, but the absence of sound. A forest is never truly quiet—there’s wind in branches, insects, distant traffic, the crack of a woodpecker somewhere off-trail. But suddenly, right around 1130, everything went still. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath. The drone’s motors were the only thing making noise, and they sounded unnaturally loud in the silence.

I landed the drone and sat back on my heels, listening. Stillness. No chickadees, no jays, no squirrels chattering in the underbrush. Nothing. My watch said 1147. I checked my phone—no signal, which wasn’t unusual, but also no emergency alerts or notifications, which usually buzz through even without bars sometimes. Just dead air.

I decided to keep moving. The trail continued northeast, climbing gently toward the ridge line. I told myself the quiet was just because I was in the middle of a clear-cut zone—no canopy cover meant fewer birds. That made sense. Rational explanations are comforting things.

The trail narrowed as I climbed. The fire scar opened up into an area where the trees had been thinned deliberately, leaving widely spaced pines that cast long shadows even though it was only noon. The ground was carpeted in pine needles, thick and spongy underfoot. My boots sank slightly with each step.

At 1230, the GPS logged its final ping. I was standing at the edge of a small clearing, maybe twenty feet across, ringed by those widely spaced trees. In the center of the clearing was a single cedar, perfectly healthy, completely out of place in the fire-damaged landscape. It stood maybe thirty feet tall with a trunk wider than my leg span, its branches hanging heavy with clusters of bright red berries that shouldn’t have been there in August.

I remember thinking that was odd. Cedars don’t usually grow this far north at this elevation. And they definitely don’t have bright red berries in August. But as I stood there looking at it, I noticed something else.

The cedar was making a sound.

Not a wind sound—my earlier observation about the lack of wind was still accurate. The tree itself seemed to be producing a low-frequency hum, like a transformer or a power line, something electrical but distant. I walked closer to investigate, maybe fifty yards from where I’d stopped, and that’s when I first noticed the cadence.

It wasn’t random. The hum came in pulses, three short bursts followed by a longer tone, then silence, then the three shorts again. Thump-thump-thump… sustained. Like a heartbeat measured in code. Or like someone tapping out a pattern in Morse, but wrong—too irregular, the intervals between the pulses varying slightly each time.

I told myself it was the drone, parked and powered off beside me, maybe still humming from residual energy. But when I walked back to check, it was completely silent. Dead electronics don’t make humming sounds.

I should have left then. Instead, I circled the clearing, keeping my distance from the cedar. The ground felt different here, too—colder than the surrounding earth, though the sun was still high. My breath started to fog slightly, which shouldn’t have happened at 58 degrees. The air around the clearing felt… thicker, somehow, like walking through a room filled with invisible dust.

At 1322, I saw it. Or rather, I saw the space where it should have been, then wasn’t.

I was walking the perimeter of the clearing, maybe ten feet from the cedar, when I felt watched. That prickling sensation on the back of my neck, the kind every hiker knows when you realize you’re not alone in the woods. I turned slowly, scanning the tree line with my eyes, then with my headlamp since my peripheral vision wasn’t catching anything solid.

Nothing there. Just trees.

But the feeling persisted. Stronger now, like pressure building behind my eyes. I turned again, slower this time, letting my light sweep methodically across trunks and branches. The beam caught something at the edge of visibility—a shape, roughly human-sized, standing between two Douglas firs about thirty yards off-trail. It was darker than the surrounding shadows, absorbed light rather than reflecting it. When I stepped forward, trying to get a better look, my headlamp flickered once and died.

I sat down hard on a fallen log, fumbling for backup batteries. My Petzl had two modes: high beam and low. When I clicked it back on, it was stuck on low. The weak circle of light barely reached the nearest tree trunks.

The silence rushed back in, but it felt different now. Heavy. Expectant. My own breathing sounded too loud. Every rustle in the underbrush, every creak of wood, seemed deliberate. Like it was listening to me.

I waited until my eyes adjusted to the dim light, then powered up my phone’s flashlight as well—both beams together gave me enough illumination to navigate. The shape was gone, but the feeling of being watched never left. It actually intensified as I backed away from the clearing, walking faster now, my pace shifting from casual hiking to purposeful retreat.

I didn’t stop until I hit the main trail again. By 1430, I was back at the pullout, my backpack heavy with gear and my mind racing with explanations. Wind in the cedar’s needles creating the humming sound. Temperature inversion making the air feel thicker. My eyes playing tricks in the low light. Exhaustion from the early start. All perfectly reasonable.

But none of them explain why the GPS stopped logging. None of them explain why, when I reviewed the drone footage later, there were seventeen seconds in the middle of the eastern face survey where the video shows nothing but static, though the drone was clearly flying based on the telemetry. None of them explain why, back at the ranger station, my supervisor looked at my tracks and said, “You should have stayed on the Connector. There’s a reason people don’t go off-trail that far east. Not since the survey crew in ’94.”

The rule I learned the hard way: in Cedar Hollow, the forest doesn’t reveal itself in clear sight. It shows you snippets—shapes at the corner of vision, sounds that almost make sense, sensations that can’t be pinned down. And the more you try to figure it out, the less sure you become about what’s real and what’s just your mind playing games with eight hours of solitude under a canopy of trees.

It’s been three weeks now. I’ve been back on short hikes, sticking to well-marked trails with good cell coverage and plenty of other people around. But I still check my six every few minutes, even when I know there’s nothing there. My friends notice it, the way I scan the tree line when we drive past any patch of woods. They think it’s trail paranoia, and maybe it is.

But here’s what really gets me: I can’t stop listening for that cadence. Three shorts, one long, then silence. I hear it in the hum of my refrigerator, in the drip of my faucet, sometimes in the rhythmic thump of my own heartbeat when I’m trying to sleep. It’s always there, just below the threshold of what I can identify as sound.

Yesterday, I went back to the trailhead. Drove past the pullout, then kept driving. But I swear—when I looked over, the brown sign was gone. Just ivy and dirt where it had been. No sign, no trail, nothing marking where the Connector should have started.

I keep my headlamp on the charger now, fully charged, ready to go. Not because I’m planning to go back. Because sometimes, late at night, I think I can hear it again, that same three-short-one-long pattern, and I want to be ready if it’s coming from outside my window instead of inside my head.

The forest doesn’t need to show itself clearly when all it has to do is make you wonder if you imagined it.
